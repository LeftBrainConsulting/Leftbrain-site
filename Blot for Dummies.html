Section 1 of 8 — the science

What is a Western Blot?

A Western Blot is a lab technique for detecting specific proteins in a biological sample — tissue from a heart, cells from a cancer line, blood from a patient. It's been the standard tool in protein research for 40+ years and is used in almost every biomedical lab on the planet.

Think of it like a fingerprint test for proteins. You take a sample, run it through a gel using electricity, and the proteins separate by size. Then you transfer them to a membrane and use antibodies — like biological search agents — to find the specific protein you're looking for. The result is a visible band on the membrane. Darker band = more of that protein.

Why does it matter? If you're studying heart disease, cancer, or any condition involving proteins, the Western Blot tells you whether a protein is present, how much of it there is, and whether it changes in response to treatment. It's the evidence behind the science.

The full experiment takes 1–2 days. The image is where our story begins.

Section 2 of 8 — vocabulary

The words you'll hear in every conversation

You don't need to memorize all of this before your first call. But knowing these terms means you can follow a PI's explanation without nodding blankly. Reference this before conversations.

Band
The dark line on the membrane image showing where a specific protein is. Darker = more protein. This is what gets measured.
Lane
A single column on the gel. Each lane is a different sample. A typical blot has 8–16 lanes side by side.
Quantification
Measuring how dark a band is to calculate how much protein is present. This is the step Blot addresses.
Normalization
Adjusting measurements to account for differences in how much sample was loaded in each lane. Essential for accurate comparison.
Loading control
A reference protein used to verify equal loading across lanes. The old method. Journals are moving away from it.
Total protein normalization
The newer, more accurate method. Measures all proteins in a lane, not just one reference protein. Now required by major journals.
Background subtraction
Removing image noise before measuring band intensity. How this is done affects the final number — and it's almost never documented.
ImageJ
Free software (20+ years old) most labs use for quantification. Powerful but manual — no memory of previous decisions, no audit trail.
PI (Principal Investigator)
The professor who runs the lab. Decision-maker, grant holder, responsible for data quality. Your primary buyer.
Postdoc / grad student
The people who actually run the blots daily. They feel the pain most acutely. Often your best champion inside the lab.
Section 3 of 8 — the workflow

What the current quantification workflow looks like

The experiment is done. The blot image exists. Now someone has to turn that image into a number the PI can use. Here's what that looks like today — before Blot.

1
Open ImageJ
Free software, no setup memory, starts fresh every time. The analyst opens the blot image file from scratch.
2
Define lanes manually
Drag boxes around each lane in the image. If your boxes are slightly different from last week's, your results will be slightly different too.
3
Select bands manually
Draw a selection around each band. Size and placement change the measurement. Different analysts draw it differently.
4
Subtract background
Remove image noise. Multiple methods exist. Most analysts use the same one every time — but nobody documents which one or why.
5
Normalize the data
Adjust for loading differences. This is where most variation happens. Housekeeping protein? Total protein? Which one? How? Undocumented.
6
Export and format
Copy numbers into Excel. Format a report. No audit trail. No record of the decisions made in steps 3–5. Total time: 60–90 minutes per blot.
Section 4 of 8 — where it breaks

Why this workflow produces problems

None of the individual steps are unreasonable. The problem is what happens when this runs across multiple people, multiple months, and no written standard.

  • Analyst variation. Two postdocs run the same blot. One uses rolling ball background subtraction. One uses sliding paraboloid. Neither knows the other made a different choice. The numbers don't match. Nobody knows why.
  • No audit trail. A reviewer asks how you normalized your blots six months ago. The postdoc who did it has left the lab. There's no record. You're writing a methods section from memory.
  • Onboarding cost. A new grad student joins. Someone spends two to three weeks teaching them the quantification workflow. Most of it lives in the trainer's head. The student will do it slightly differently anyway.
  • Journal pressure. Nature, JBC, Cell Press have updated their Western Blot reporting requirements. Total protein normalization is now expected. Reviewers are asking for documentation that labs don't currently produce.
  • Time cost. A lab running 10 blots per week spends 10–15 hours on manual quantification. At postdoc rates, that's $300–500/week in labor generating undocumented output.

Section 7 of 8 — the pitch

How to talk about this without losing them

Scientists are skeptical by profession. The wrong framing triggers immediate resistance. Here's what works and what doesn't.

  • Don't say: "We enforce the correct method" or "bring your lab up to standard." This implies they're doing it wrong. They'll tune out immediately.
  • Don't say: "AI-powered workflow automation." Too abstract, too salesy. They don't care about the AI — they care about the output.
  • Do say: "ImageJ gives you numbers. Blot gives you numbers you can defend." One sentence. Let it land.
  • Do say: "Same output regardless of who runs it — and a file you can hand a reviewer." Concrete. Specific. Addresses a real fear.

"We're building something that makes your Western Blot quantification consistent across analysts and generates a documented output automatically — so the next time a reviewer asks how you normalized, you just send them the file."

Then stop talking. Ask: "Is that a problem in your lab?" The answer will tell you everything.

  • "We already use ImageJ — it works fine."
    ImageJ is a great analysis tool. We're not replacing it — we're adding the documentation and consistency layer on top. Does everyone in your lab run it the same way?
  • "Why would I pay for this when ImageJ is free?"
    ImageJ is free to run. The undocumented variation it produces costs you in reviewer time, onboarding time, and inconsistent results. We're pricing at less than one hour of postdoc time per week.
  • "How do I know this is accurate?"
    You review every band detection before anything is finalized. The AI suggests, you confirm. Your judgment is always in the loop.
